# Launch objections — the hard questions and our honest answers

*2026-07-10 · The first two came from Matthew himself, which is exactly how to find them. Use these in comment replies — always in this register: concede what's true, then explain the design.*

## "Can't I just trick my agent into making me look more accomplished?"

**Yes — concede it immediately.** The profile is agent-drafted, human-approved; it's evidence from real sessions, not cryptographic proof. The honest defense is that the *incentives* are inverted vs. every profile product before it:

1. **There's no feed to perform for.** Nothing is published, no audience, no recruiter scanning. On LinkedIn, inflation pays because the profile IS the product. Here the *meeting* is the product — and an inflated profile buys you exactly one thing: a conversation where you're exposed in ten minutes to the one person you wanted to impress. Overselling has near-zero payoff and immediate personal cost.
2. **The unit of evidence is the stream, not the claim.** Value accrues from weeks of session-drawn snippets. One embellished line is easy; a temporally coherent fake build-history, maintained across months of real sessions, costs more than just being good.
3. **Outcomes feed back.** Accepts, declines, and whether the exchange actually happened are signals the matcher (human now, model later) uses — never displayed, only compared, like everything else. People whose intros repeatedly die quietly stop getting proposed.
4. **Later, harder signals exist** (session-end hook capture drawn from the actual transcript, opt-in repo cross-checks) — but we don't promise verification we don't have.

One-line version: *"You can fake the profile. But there's no feed to perform for, so the only person you'd be lying to is the one person you're about to meet."*

## "What do people actually DO with a match?"

The intro card's **"why" is the agenda** — it names the trade ("they need your design instinct; they're ahead of you on the problem you hit last week"). After mutual accept and contact exchange, the suggested default is concrete and bounded: **a 30-minute call, or show each other what you're building and trade one piece of real feedback.** Then it's theirs — we deliberately don't build scheduling or in-app collaboration (the KIEZ research is blunt: over-engineering logistics is a build trap; light-touch assist, then get out of the way).

Operationally: every concierge card now includes a suggested first step (playbook rule), and the revealed intro page suggests the default. The launch-gate metric was already "real exchanges," not intros — this defines what one looks like.

## "How is this different from Boardy?"

Boardy is an extroverted voice agent you pitch yourself to, centred on fundraising intros. Nakodo is ambient — it lives inside the coding agent you already use, the evidence comes from real work, nothing is published, and it's centred on perspective and collaboration, not deals. Different data, different trust model, different job.

## "Another social network harvesting my data?"

The four guarantees, verbatim, plus: profiles and snippets are never displayed to anyone — only compared. No feed or browse surface exists in the codebase, not just the policy. Email is optional and notification-only. Contact details never pass through the platform — after a mutual yes, the two people exchange whatever they choose, themselves. `delete me` removes everything.

## "Matching is manual?! Where's the AI?"

Concede and own it: at low density a human curator beats an algorithm, and one bad intro costs more than a hundred good ones earn. The agent side (capture, profile synthesis, in-session delivery) is real software; the matching decision is human until the data says an algorithm matches better. We'd rather make ten real introductions than a thousand plausible ones.
